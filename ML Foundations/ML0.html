ML Foundations#

The models, the evaluation discipline, and the data handling that everything after this assumes. Deep learning (Part IV) is a specialization of these ideas, not a replacement for them — and in production, the models in this part still do more real work than neural networks do.

Coming from a data science role? Skim, don’t skip.#

If you’ve been doing applied DS or experimentation work, most of this is already yours. That’s genuinely an advantage — plenty of MLE candidates are weaker here than you are.

Self-check. If you can answer all of these comfortably, skim ML1–ML6 and read ML7 properly:

  1. Why does L1 regularization produce exact zeros when L2 doesn’t?

  2. Why must you fit a scaler on the training split only — and what specifically leaks if you don’t?

  3. What does bagging reduce, what does boosting reduce, and why does that difference matter?

  4. When does a high ROC-AUC coexist with a useless model?

  5. Why do tree-based models not need feature scaling?

If several of those are shaky, read the chapter in question in full.

What is probably new, regardless of your background — and where this part is worth your time even if the algorithms aren’t:

  • ML7 as a whole. You know precision and recall. What’s likely new is choosing an operating threshold against a business constraint, calibration as a distinct property from ranking quality, and connecting an offline metric to an online one you can actually move.

  • The “What changes in production” section closing each chapter. This is the transition material: a StandardScaler in a notebook is a line of code, but in production it is a versioned artifact that must be reproduced exactly at serving time or your model silently degrades. The algorithms don’t change when you deploy them. Everything around them does.

How this part connects#

  • Part V (ML Coding interviews) re-implements much of this from scratch in NumPy. ML1 → mlc1, ML5/ML6 → mlc2, ML7 → mlc5. Learn it here, implement it there.

  • Part IV (Deep Learning) builds directly on ML1’s loss/gradient-descent material and ML2’s overfitting material. DL3 is ML2’s regularization section specialized to neural networks.

  • Part VII (ML System Design) assumes all of it. mlsd3’s “GBDT vs deep models” is unreadable without ML5.

Key Takeaways#

  • These models are still the production default for tabular problems — gradient-boosted trees remain the thing to beat, and often aren’t beaten

  • Evaluation discipline (ML2) and leakage avoidance (ML3) transfer unchanged to deep learning and LLMs

  • If you’re strong here already, spend your time on ML7 and the production-delta sections
